# Type 2 Diabetes: Current Evidence and Research Priorities

Prepared October 31, 2026  
Status: initial rapid evidence brief

## Executive summary

Type 2 diabetes is common, frequently undiagnosed, and strongly linked to cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, vision loss, neuropathy, and premature death. The CDC estimates that 40.1 million people in the United States had diagnosed or undiagnosed diabetes in 2023, including 11.0 million adults who were unaware of their condition. It estimates that 115.2 million U.S. adults had prediabetes. These figures should not be compared directly with earlier CDC reports because the 2026 report changed its glucose-calibration methodology.

The central conclusion from current guidance is that type 2 diabetes care can no longer be reduced to lowering A1C. Effective care combines individualized glycemic goals with weight management, cardiovascular and kidney protection, avoidance of hypoglycemia, complication screening, education, affordability, and patient preference.

For many nonpregnant adults, an A1C goal below 7% is a reasonable starting point, but it should be individualized. Lower goals may suit people with good health and low treatment risk; less stringent goals may be safer for people with frailty, significant comorbidity, limited functional capacity, or high hypoglycemia burden.

Modern medication selection is driven by comorbidity as much as by glucose. In people with established or high cardiovascular risk, heart failure, or chronic kidney disease, an SGLT2 inhibitor and/or GLP-1 receptor agonist with demonstrated outcome benefit may be indicated regardless of current A1C or metformin use. Weight is also a primary treatment target when overweight or obesity is present. Cost, adverse effects, contraindications, and access can substantially change the best practical choice.

## 1. Population burden

The CDC's March 2026 National Diabetes Statistics Report estimates, using 2023 population data:

- 40.1 million people of all ages had diabetes, or 12.0% of the U.S. population.
- 39.8 million adults had diabetes, or 14.9% of U.S. adults.
- 11.0 million adults had undiagnosed diabetes, representing 27.6% of adults with diabetes.
- 115.2 million adults had prediabetes.
- Diabetes was the seventh leading underlying cause of death in 2023, appearing as the underlying cause on 95,190 death certificates.

The burden is uneven. In age-adjusted 2021–2023 estimates, total adult diabetes prevalence varied by race, ethnicity, and education. These differences should be interpreted as markers of unequal risk and unequal social and health-system conditions—not biological destiny. The surveillance estimates are also limited by their reliance on survey samples, laboratory definitions, self-report, and the assumption that survey-period prevalence applies to the 2023 population.

Source: [CDC National Diabetes Statistics Report (2026)](https://usdss.cdc.gov/diabetes/data/statsreport/National_Diabetes_Statsitics_Report.pdf)

## 2. Screening and diagnosis

The 2026 ADA criteria for diabetes in nonpregnant people are any of the following:

- A1C at least 6.5%
- Fasting plasma glucose at least 126 mg/dL after at least eight hours without caloric intake
- Two-hour plasma glucose at least 200 mg/dL during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test
- Random plasma glucose at least 200 mg/dL in a person with classic hyperglycemic symptoms or hyperglycemic crisis

Unless hyperglycemia is unequivocal, diagnosis requires two abnormal results, either from different tests obtained together or from repeat testing. A1C can be misleading in conditions that alter red-cell turnover or hemoglobin, and discordance between A1C and plasma glucose warrants investigation.

For asymptomatic adults without another indication, routine screening begins at age 35. Earlier testing is recommended for adults with overweight or obesity plus at least one risk factor. People with prediabetes should generally be tested yearly; people with normal results should usually be retested at least every three years, sooner if risk changes.

Source: [ADA, Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes)

## 3. Prevention and remission

For people at high risk, prevention is not a vague instruction to “live healthier.” The strongest behavioral programs are structured, intensive, and supported over time. Clinically meaningful targets include approximately 5–7% weight loss and regular physical activity. The ADA recommends at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity for most adults with diabetes, spread across at least three days, plus resistance exercise two or three times weekly.

Metformin can be considered for diabetes prevention in selected high-risk adults, especially those aged 25–59 with BMI at least 35 kg/m², higher fasting glucose or A1C, or prior gestational diabetes. This is targeted prevention rather than universal drug treatment for prediabetes.

In established type 2 diabetes with overweight or obesity, sustained loss of 5–7% of body weight improves glycemia and intermediate cardiovascular risk factors. Loss above 10% generally produces larger metabolic benefits and can enable remission in some people, particularly earlier in the disease course. “Remission” does not mean cure: glycemia and complications still require monitoring, and relapse remains possible.

Sources: [ADA, Prevention or Delay of Diabetes—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S50/163924/3-Prevention-or-Delay-of-Diabetes-and-Associated), [ADA, Positive Health Behaviors—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S89/163932), and [ADA, Obesity and Weight Management—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S166/163915/8-Obesity-and-Weight-Management-for-the-Prevention)

## 4. Glycemic goals and monitoring

For many nonpregnant adults, the ADA recommends an A1C target below 7%. A lower target, such as below 6.5%, may be appropriate when it can be achieved with low risk and low burden. Less stringent goals are appropriate when treatment harms or burdens outweigh likely benefit. Avoiding severe or recurrent hypoglycemia takes priority over meeting a numerical target.

For many people using continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), a time-in-range goal above 70% is a useful starting point. The 2026 ADA standards recommend CGM for adults on insulin, on non-insulin therapies that can cause hypoglycemia, and in other circumstances where CGM meaningfully aids management. Benefit depends on access, training, the ability to interpret data, and whether the information changes care.

Sources: [ADA, Glycemic Goals—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S132/163927/6-Glycemic-Goals-Hypoglycemia-and-Hyperglycemic) and [ADA, Diabetes Technology—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S150/163922)

## 5. Medication strategy

Medication choice should be person-centered and outcome-specific.

### When cardiovascular or kidney disease drives the decision

- Established or high risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease: use a GLP-1 receptor agonist and/or SGLT2 inhibitor with demonstrated cardiovascular benefit, irrespective of A1C.
- Heart failure: an SGLT2 inhibitor is recommended to reduce heart-failure hospitalization, irrespective of A1C.
- Chronic kidney disease with eGFR 20–60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or albuminuria: use an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 receptor agonist with demonstrated benefit to slow kidney progression and reduce cardiovascular events.
- Advanced CKD: drug choice and dosing require particular care; the ADA favors suitable GLP-1–based therapy for glycemic management when eGFR is below 30 because of lower hypoglycemia risk and cardiovascular benefit.

### When weight and glycemia drive the decision

GLP-1 receptor agonists and the dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist tirzepatide have high glucose-lowering and weight-loss efficacy, but gastrointestinal effects, contraindications, long-term treatment burden, access, and cost matter. SGLT2 inhibitors and metformin generally have more modest weight effects. Sulfonylureas and insulin remain effective glucose-lowering tools but have greater hypoglycemia risk and commonly cause weight gain.

In adults without severe hyperglycemia or crisis, the ADA generally prefers GLP-1–based therapy before insulin as the initial injectable or add-on option. Insulin should be considered when hyperglycemia is symptomatic or very high, including A1C above 10% or blood glucose at least 300 mg/dL, especially with weight loss, ketosis, or other catabolic features.

These are class-level organizing principles, not interchangeable prescriptions. Outcome evidence, adverse effects, kidney clearance, labeling, and insurance coverage differ among individual drugs.

Source: [ADA, Pharmacologic Approaches—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S183/163934)

## 6. Cardiovascular and kidney risk reduction

Cardiovascular risk reduction extends beyond glucose-lowering drugs. Blood pressure, smoking, lipids, physical activity, nutrition, and kidney status must be managed together. The ADA recommends at least moderate-intensity statin therapy for most people with diabetes aged 40–75 without established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, with high-intensity therapy for higher-risk primary prevention and for secondary prevention when tolerated.

Kidney screening should include both eGFR and a spot urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio at least annually in every person with type 2 diabetes. Measuring only serum creatinine can miss clinically important albuminuria. People with established CKD require monitoring one to four times yearly according to stage and progression risk.

Sources: [ADA, Cardiovascular Disease and Risk Management—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S216/163933/10-Cardiovascular-Disease-and-Risk-Management) and [ADA, Chronic Kidney Disease and Risk Management—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S246/163914/11-Chronic-Kidney-Disease-and-Risk-Management)

## 7. Complication surveillance

Complication prevention requires scheduled surveillance, not symptom-triggered testing alone.

- Assess peripheral neuropathy starting at type 2 diabetes diagnosis and at least annually thereafter.
- Perform annual 10-g monofilament testing to identify feet at risk for ulceration or amputation.
- Complete a comprehensive foot evaluation at least annually; inspect feet at every visit when protective sensation is lost or there is a history of ulceration or amputation.
- Monitor kidney function and albuminuria at least annually.
- Include retinal assessment on the recommended clinical schedule, with frequency based on existing retinopathy and glycemic risk.

Source: [ADA, Retinopathy, Neuropathy, and Foot Care—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-lookup/doi/10.2337/dc26-S012)

## 8. What is well established

- Structured lifestyle intervention can delay or prevent type 2 diabetes in high-risk people.
- Lowering chronic hyperglycemia reduces microvascular complications, but goals must be balanced against hypoglycemia and treatment burden.
- SGLT2 inhibitors and selected GLP-1 receptor agonists can reduce cardiovascular and/or kidney events in defined high-risk populations; their value is not captured by A1C change alone.
- Weight loss improves glycemia and cardiometabolic risk, with larger sustained loss usually producing greater benefit.
- Statins, blood-pressure management, smoking cessation, and kidney surveillance are core diabetes care rather than optional add-ons.
- Regular foot, neuropathy, kidney, and eye surveillance can identify preventable complications before advanced symptoms appear.

## 9. Important uncertainties and evidence gaps

1. **Long-term comparative effectiveness.** Direct head-to-head evidence among modern GLP-1–based therapies, SGLT2 inhibitors, combination strategies, and metabolic surgery remains incomplete for mortality, kidney failure, quality of life, and durability.
2. **Real-world access and persistence.** Trial efficacy may not translate when cost, shortages, insurance changes, adverse effects, or complex regimens interrupt treatment.
3. **Weight-loss maintenance.** Weight regain after stopping pharmacotherapy is common. Evidence is limited on optimal maintenance dosing, intermittent treatment, and the best transition strategy after discontinuation.
4. **CGM outside insulin treatment.** Glycemic benefits are increasingly supported, but long-term patient-important outcomes and cost-effectiveness across lower-risk populations need clearer definition.
5. **Remission durability.** More evidence is needed on who sustains remission, how remission affects long-term complication risk, and how often monitoring should occur.
6. **Underrepresented populations.** Older adults with multimorbidity, people with low income or unstable insurance, rural populations, and some racial and ethnic groups remain underrepresented in many trials.
7. **Treatment sequencing.** Guidelines describe preferred classes, but the best sequence and combination for a particular mix of obesity, CKD, heart failure, liver disease, hypoglycemia risk, and affordability is not fully resolved.

1. [Diabetes prevention and remission](pivotal-evidence-prevention-remission.md) — initial extraction completed November 10, 2026
2. [GLP-1/GIP-based therapies and metabolic surgery](pivotal-evidence-incretins-surgery.md) for weight and glycemic outcomes — initial extraction completed November 10, 2026
3. [SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists for cardiovascular and kidney outcomes](pivotal-evidence-cardiorenal.md) — initial extraction completed November 10, 2026

5. Implementation, cost, adherence, and disparities

Each module should report population, intervention, comparator, follow-up, absolute and relative effects, adverse events, applicability, and risk of bias. This will allow the final report to distinguish what improves laboratory markers from what improves survival, complications, function, and quality of life.

## Limitations of this brief

This is a rapid orientation based primarily on the 2026 ADA Standards of Care and the 2026 CDC surveillance report. It is not yet a full systematic review, has not extracted every pivotal trial, and does not provide patient-specific recommendations. Clinical decisions require review of the complete guideline, current drug labeling, comorbidities, contraindications, preferences, and access.
