# Pivotal evidence module 4: continuous glucose monitoring in type 2 diabetes and Stelo interpretation

Updated: November 10, 2026  
Scope: randomized evidence for continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) in adults with type 2 diabetes using basal insulin or no insulin, current guideline interpretation, and device-specific measurement limits relevant to an over-the-counter Stelo experiment. This is an evidence extraction and measurement guide, not individualized medical advice.

## Bottom line

- CGM improves A1C and time in range in adults with inadequately controlled type 2 diabetes using basal insulin. Newer randomized evidence also supports benefit in adults with type 2 diabetes not using insulin, particularly when baseline A1C is high and the data are paired with education and treatment decisions.
- The trials studied diabetes management, not healthy people optimizing isolated post-meal peaks. They do not establish that minimizing every glucose excursion improves cardiovascular or kidney outcomes.
- Stelo is cleared for adults not using insulin and without problematic hypoglycemia. It has no glucose alarms, displays only 70–250 mg/dL numerically, and is not intended to drive medical action without consultation with a qualified health professional.
- CGM measures interstitial glucose and has measurement error. It is useful for repeated within-person patterns, but one sensor trace cannot diagnose diabetes, determine insulin resistance, or prove that a food caused a long-term clinical effect.
- For the planned N-of-1 study, the most defensible claim is narrow: under standardized conditions, did a randomized 10-minute post-dinner walk change the two-hour glucose response compared with remaining sedentary?

## Randomized evidence in basal-insulin-treated type 2 diabetes

| Study | Population and design | Follow-up | Main findings | Interpretation boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [MOBILE](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34077499/) (2021) | 175 adults with type 2 diabetes treated with one or two daily basal-insulin injections, without prandial insulin, in 15 U.S. primary care centers; real-time CGM versus blood-glucose meter monitoring | 8 months | Baseline A1C was about 9.1%. A1C fell to 8.0% with CGM and 8.4% with meter monitoring (adjusted difference −0.4 percentage points, 95% CI −0.8 to −0.1). Time 70–180 mg/dL was 59% versus 43%, and time above 250 mg/dL was 11% versus 27%. One severe hypoglycemia event occurred in each group. | Strong evidence for people with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes using basal insulin. It does not answer Stelo use because Stelo is not intended for insulin users, nor does it establish benefit in people without diabetes or with near-normal A1C. Dexcom participated and funded the trial. |

## Randomized evidence in non-insulin-treated type 2 diabetes

| Study | Population and design | Follow-up | Main findings | Interpretation boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [IMMEDIATE](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36546594/) (2023) | 116 adults using at least one non-insulin therapy, mean A1C 8.6% and diabetes duration 10 years; intermittently scanned CGM plus diabetes self-management education versus education alone | 16 weeks | In the final two weeks, CGM increased time 70–180 mg/dL by 9.9 percentage points, or 2.4 hours/day, reduced time above range by 8.1 points, and reduced A1C by 0.3 points more than education alone. Time below range was low in both groups. | Small, short, open-label trial. It tests CGM plus structured education in established, inadequately controlled diabetes—not passive observation, Stelo specifically, or use without diabetes. Industry funded. |
| [Ssemmondo et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39663609/) (2025) | 40 adults with type 2 diabetes using non-insulin therapies; visible intermittently scanned CGM versus blinded professional CGM | 12 weeks | The prespecified A1C responder outcome was not statistically different (53% versus 35%, P=0.34). Visible CGM improved time in range by 18 percentage points (95% CI 2–35); the between-group A1C change was not significant. | Pilot study with wide uncertainty. It supports a possible feedback effect but is too small for a reliable effect-size estimate. |
| [CONNECT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42878622/) (2026) | 283 adults with type 2 diabetes not using insulin at 22 U.S. primary care practices; continuous Dexcom G7 use versus routine care | 26 weeks | A1C fell from 8.8% to 7.2% with CGM and from 8.7% to 8.0% with routine care (adjusted difference −0.9 points, 95% CI −1.1 to −0.6). Time 70–180 mg/dL was 21 percentage points higher with CGM. No severe hypoglycemia was reported. | Largest and most recent fully powered trial in this population, but it studied G7—not Stelo—and people with established diabetes and high baseline A1C. The result reflects the complete feedback-and-care pathway, not a direct biological effect of wearing a sensor. Funded by Dexcom. |

## Systematic-review context

- A [2024 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38090767/) pooled six randomized trials with 407 non-insulin-treated participants. Compared with blood-glucose meter monitoring, CGM reduced A1C by 0.31 percentage points (95% CI 0.21–0.42), increased time 70–180 mg/dL by 8.63 points, and reduced time above 180 mg/dL by 7.75 points. Small trials, short follow-up, different devices, and intervention heterogeneity limit precision and durability claims.
- A broader [2024 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38363342/) found an A1C difference of about −0.31 percentage points overall, with similar average estimates in insulin-treated and oral-agent-only studies. Meta-analysis does not remove the limitations of open-label behavior interventions or prove clinical-event benefit.
- CONNECT is newer and much larger than the trials in those reviews. Its effect estimate is correspondingly influential but still requires replication, longer follow-up, and independent evaluation of durability and implementation.

## Guideline position and the diagnosis boundary

The [ADA Standards of Care 2026 technology section](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S150/163922/7-Diabetes-Technology-Standards-of-Care-in) recommends CGM for people with diabetes on insulin, on non-insulin therapies that can cause hypoglycemia, and on any diabetes treatment where CGM helps management. It emphasizes individualization, education, interpretation, and continued access rather than treating the device as a standalone intervention.

For people with diabetes, the [ADA glycemic-goals section](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S132/163927/6-Glycemic-Goals-Hypoglycemia-and-Hyperglycemic) says a 10- to 14-day assessment with at least 70% active data can be used for pattern management. A common goal for many nonpregnant adults with diabetes is more than 70% of time from 70–180 mg/dL, but goals must be individualized. This target is not a diagnostic cutoff and should not be imported uncritically into people without diabetes.

The [ADA diagnosis section](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes) explicitly says evidence is insufficient to use CGM to screen for or diagnose prediabetes or diabetes. Diagnosis relies on validated laboratory A1C, fasting plasma glucose, two-hour oral glucose tolerance testing, or random plasma glucose with classic symptoms, with confirmatory testing when hyperglycemia is not unequivocal.

## What is specific to Stelo

The [FDA decision summary for Stelo](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/K234070.pdf) defines both its intended use and its measurement limits:

| Feature | FDA-cleared characteristic | Research implication |
|---|---|---|
| Intended population | Adults age 18 or older not using insulin; do not use with problematic hypoglycemia or dialysis | The device is not a safety monitor for insulin dosing or clinically important hypoglycemia. |
| Intended action | May help users understand how diet and exercise affect glucose excursions; medical action should not be taken from device output without a qualified health professional | Use the experiment to generate questions and behavioral evidence, not to self-diagnose or change medication. |
| Measurement | Interstitial glucose via a factory-calibrated sensor; no optional user calibration | A fingerstick cannot recalibrate Stelo. Treat a discordant point as measurement uncertainty, not something to “correct” in the app. |
| Display | Sensor information reaches the app every five minutes, but readings are displayed every 15 minutes; values below 70 appear as “Below 70” and above 250 as “Above 250” | Exact peaks and nadirs can be interval-censored on the visible trace. Preserve exported data and the device's flags. |
| Alerts | No urgent-low, low, high, or rate-of-change alarms | Do not rely on Stelo for immediate detection of dangerous glucose changes. Symptoms and clinical guidance take priority. |
| Accuracy | In the FDA study, 93.1% of 20,330 matched values were within 20% of the laboratory comparator across 40–400 mg/dL. MARD was 9.1% early, 7.8% and 7.3% in the middle periods, and 9.1% near the end | Single readings can differ materially from reference glucose. Repeated paired comparisons and integrated response are more defensible than declaring one isolated peak “good” or “bad.” |
| Sensor survival | In the FDA study, 73.5% of 131 evaluable sensors lasted to the final day; 19.8% had early sensor shutoff | Missingness and premature sensor failure must be logged. Do not replace missing values by visual interpolation in the primary analysis. |
| Interference and procedures | Hydroxyurea can falsely raise readings; acetaminophen above 1 g every six hours may falsely raise readings; device-specific restrictions apply to MRI, diathermy, and CT positioning | Record relevant medications and procedures and follow the current product instructions. Exclude materially affected periods from the primary experiment. |

## Analysis rules for the planned Stelo N-of-1 study

The complete protocol is in [stelo-n-of-1-protocol.md](stelo-n-of-1-protocol.md). The evidence above supports these interpretation rules:

1. **Keep the causal question narrow.** The randomized comparison is a 10-minute post-dinner walk versus remaining sedentary under otherwise standardized conditions. It is not a trial of “healthy living,” diabetes prevention, or cardiovascular risk.
2. **Prefer repeated within-sensor contrasts.** Systematic sensor bias is less damaging when randomized conditions are interleaved on the same sensor. Sensor sequence, day of wear, and condition order should still be retained.
3. **Use net iAUC 0–120 minutes as the primary meal outcome.** It uses the whole response curve relative to a premeal baseline and is less sensitive to a single noisy point than peak glucose alone.
4. **Treat peak rise, time to peak, two-hour change, and time above a prespecified threshold as supportive outcomes.** Do not choose the most favorable metric after seeing the data.
5. **Do not use the first 24 hours for the primary randomized phase.** FDA accuracy was somewhat worse during days 1–3 than the middle wear period; an observational acclimation period also prevents learning and setup errors from contaminating treatment comparisons.
6. **Preserve raw exports before cleaning.** Save the unmodified file, record its SHA-256 hash, and create a separate analysis copy. Log missing intervals, signal loss, early shutdown, illness, unusual exercise, meal deviations, alcohol, and relevant medication changes.
7. **Do not diagnose from the trace.** Repeated unexpected patterns can justify discussing validated laboratory testing with a clinician. They do not establish diabetes, prediabetes, insulin resistance, or a need for medication.
8. **Separate statistical from clinical interpretation.** Even a reproducible reduction in two-hour glucose exposure is a short-term physiological/behavioral finding. This experiment cannot demonstrate fewer heart attacks, kidney events, or diabetes diagnoses.

## What the evidence can and cannot support

| Supported claim | Unsupported leap |
|---|---|
| CGM plus feedback and education can improve glycemic management in selected adults with type 2 diabetes. | Wearing a sensor alone treats diabetes. |
| Stelo can reveal repeatable within-person responses to meals and activity, subject to measurement error. | One high post-meal value diagnoses disease or predicts a future complication. |
| Randomized repeated meal comparisons can estimate a short-term effect of a post-meal walk for this user under the tested conditions. | The result applies to all meals, all people, or long-term clinical outcomes. |
| A full sensor period can describe patterns and data quality. | A single sensor precisely estimates a 90-day A1C or establishes a universal “optimal” glucose range for a person without diabetes. |

## Evidence judgment

1. **Basal insulin:** high confidence that real-time CGM improves glycemic metrics in adults with inadequately controlled type 2 diabetes using basal insulin.
2. **No insulin:** moderate-to-high and strengthening confidence that CGM improves short-term A1C and time in range in adults with inadequately controlled type 2 diabetes not using insulin; durability, cost-effectiveness, and which implementation components drive benefit remain less certain.
3. **Prediabetes or no diabetes:** insufficient evidence for diagnostic use or for claiming that reducing CGM excursions improves clinical outcomes.
4. **Stelo N-of-1:** appropriate as a carefully logged behavioral experiment and hypothesis generator, not as a diagnostic or treatment study.
